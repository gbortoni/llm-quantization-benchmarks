The Mexican Revolution began in 1910 after decades of political stability under Porfirio Díaz had produced economic growth alongside deep social and political tensions. Díaz had remained in power for many years, and opposition groups increasingly demanded political participation and limits on presidential reelection. Francisco I. Madero emerged as a leading opponent and called for an uprising after the disputed presidential election of 1910.

The revolution soon developed into a much broader conflict involving leaders, armies, and regional movements with different goals. In northern Mexico, figures such as Pancho Villa became important military leaders, while Emiliano Zapata led a movement centered in Morelos that demanded land reform and defended the interests of rural communities. Zapata's followers promoted the Plan de Ayala, which called for redistribution of land and criticized governments that failed to address agrarian inequality.

Madero became president after Díaz left power, but his government faced opposition from several directions. In 1913, Madero was overthrown and killed during a military coup associated with Victoriano Huerta. Opposition to Huerta brought together revolutionary forces led by Venustiano Carranza, Álvaro Obregón, Villa, and Zapata, although their alliance did not last.
